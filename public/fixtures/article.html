Notes on reading two texts at once

A fixture page. Every construct here exists to exercise one rule.

Reading a translation beside its original is an old habit. Scribes ruled two columns on a single sheet so the eye could cross between them without losing its place, and the practice survives in facing-page editions of poetry. What changes on the web is that the two texts no longer agree on how much room they need.

A paragraph that runs four lines in English may run three in Japanese, or six. Any attempt to keep the two columns aligned by scrolling them in proportion drifts a little further apart with every screen. Alignment has to be anchored to the content itself.

What the panes must agree on

  • The order of the blocks, so an anchor in one pane names a block in the other.
  • The identity of each block, which survives translation only if nothing is reparented.
  • Nothing else. Heights, line counts and wrapping are all free to differ.
The eye does not read a page. It reads a line, and remembers roughly where the line sat.

Constructs that must be left alone

An inline reference to Array.prototype.flatMap is not prose, and neither is a block of source: 

function followerScrollTop(leaderTop, leaderAnchor, followerAnchor) {
  return followerAnchor + (leaderTop - leaderAnchor);
}

This paragraph carries translate="no" and must survive untouched, in every language.

This one uses the notranslate class, which Google Translate has honoured for years.

An icon ligature such as menu_open is decorative, and translating it would replace a glyph with a word.

Numbers and symbols carry no letters at all: 1,234.56 — 98% — © 2026 — →. None of them should cost a translation call.

Enough length to scroll

The passage below exists so the page is taller than one screen at any reasonable split ratio. Scroll synchronisation cannot be judged on a page that does not scroll.

When a reader moves down a column, the thing they are tracking is not a pixel offset but a sentence. Restoring that sentence to the same height in the facing column is the whole of the problem, and it is why the anchor is taken a fifth of the way down the viewport rather than at its very top: a block straddling the top edge is one the reader has already finished.

Sites that render differently on each load — an advertisement slot, a rotating quotation, a timestamp counted from now — break the assumption that two fetches of one URL produce one structure. Where that happens the panes cannot be paired, and saying so plainly is better than silently falling back to a proportional scroll that will drift.

The remaining paragraphs say nothing in particular. They are here to be long.

Consider how much of a page is not prose: navigation, breadcrumbs, cookie notices, share buttons, bylines, timestamps, tag lists, related-article teasers, and the footer's list of corporate links. A translator that walks every text node spends most of its budget on these before it reaches the first sentence of the article, which is the argument for translating what is in view first and letting the rest arrive behind it.

Consider also how many of those fragments repeat. The word "Home" appears once in the header and once in the footer; a table's column heading repeats down a list of cards. Memoising on the trimmed string collapses them into a single call, and on an ordinary article that removes something between a sixth and a third of the work.

The last thing worth saying is that none of this needs a network. The model runs on the machine, which means a page in a hotel with no signal translates exactly as fast as one at a desk, and no sentence of what is being read leaves the device.
